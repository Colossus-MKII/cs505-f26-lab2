ASAP7. At both 256 and 2,048 B the single-cycle path starts at the fetch PC and ends at register writeback. The launch flip-flop's clock-to-Q contribution grows from 0.529 to 3.820 ns as its address output drives a larger selection network. At the intermediate sizes the longest path ends at a RAM write instead. The three-stage bottleneck changes from the EX-to-PC redirect to PC-to-EX-instruction fetch. The multi-cycle bottleneck stays within the controller, with periods of 0.857 and 0.864 ns at the two endpoints.

Nangate45. Single-cycle again runs from PC to a register-file endpoint. Its launch delay alone rises from 3.852 to 34.488 ns, accounting for most of the 40.202 ns period at 2,048 B. The pipeline changes from EX instruction to PC at 256 B to a PC-to-PC next-address path at 2,048 B. The latter starts on a heavily loaded fetch-address bit (11.456 ns clock-to-Q). The multi-cycle controller stays near 2.4–2.5 ns.

Sky130 HD. Single-cycle changes from PC-to-GPR writeback to PC-to-RAM write. The multi-cycle path changes from controller-state feedback to controller-state-to-RAM write. The pipeline stays PC-to-EX-instruction fetch, but grows from 20.082 to 145.308 ns. Single-cycle has the largest absolute period increase at all three nodes. Measured proportionally, the Sky130 pipeline is more sensitive, at 7.24 times its initial period versus 6.48 times for single-cycle. The launch delays and endpoint changes explain why increasing capacity need not add the same delay to every design.
